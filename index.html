Your first page

25 min · nothing to install

Six keys, one scene, a real page. Do this before you read anything else here.

A screenplay page is not a document with margins you set. It is six kinds of line, and the page knows which one you are on. You press Enter and Tab; it does the indenting, the capitals and the spacing. That is the whole mechanical skill, and it takes about twenty minutes to stop thinking about it.

Open the page

Press Write at the top, or the button here. A blank page opens with the cursor on a scene heading and a coach that ticks off each key as you use it.

The six keys, in the order you will need them

  1. Type the place and time. INT. LAUNDROMAT - NIGHT. Typing int. at the start of a line turns it into a scene heading on its own — you do not have to choose anything.
  2. Enter → you are on an action line. Describe only what a camera could photograph, in the present tense. A dryer thumps. NADIA watches the door.
  3. Enter then Tab → a character cue, centred and capitalised. Type NADIA.
  4. Enter → dialogue, indented under the name. Type what she says.
  5. Enter then Tab → back out to action, or Tab again for a transition. Every shape is two keys from every other one.
  6. Ctrl or Alt + 1–6 forces a line to be a heading, action, character, parenthetical, dialogue or transition when the machine guessed wrong.

Those six keys are the same in Final Draft, so nothing you learn here has to be unlearned later. Ctrl+Z undoes, Alt+J leaves a note that never prints, and Esc closes the page and brings you back here.

Now write one scene

Not a good one. A real one. Two people, one room, one of them wants something the other will not give. Half a page is enough — stop when the thing they wanted is settled either way. If you cannot think of anyone, use this: someone returns a borrowed object, and does not want to leave.

When you have it: Doctor reads your pages against the rules that a reader notices first, and Script ▾ puts a PDF in your hands, or an .fdx that opens in Final Draft. That is a finished thing, an hour after starting from nothing.

The page in six shapes

Once, tonight or tomorrow · 20 min

Everything on a screenplay page is one of six things.

A screenplay page has exactly six kinds of line on it. Six. Once you can name all six and see which is which, you can read any script in the world and you know what every line on your own page is doing.

The three facts

  1. A screenplay contains only what a camera can SEE and a microphone can HEAR. No thoughts, no feelings, no backstory. "Maya is nervous" — unfilmable. "Maya wipes her palms on her skirt. Checks her reflection twice." — filmable. That one swap is half the craft.
  2. 1 page ≈ 1 minute of screen time. The layout is strict (Courier 12, fixed margins, ~55 lines per page) because the strictness makes the math work.
  3. You never invent the layout — you copy it. Only six shapes of text exist on any script page. the page presses the margins; you press Enter and Tab (or Ctrl+number to force an element → Writing the pages).

Four mini-scenes, four non-obvious rules

A — The Camera Test (no inner states — behavior carries everything)

INT. KITCHEN - MORNING

DAVID (40s, still in yesterday's clothes) pours coffee.
Stares at the full cup. Doesn't drink.

His phone lights up on the counter: MOM CALLING.

He turns it face-down.

Pours the coffee into the sink.

David's entire emotional state is in behavior: yesterday's clothes, the untouched cup, the rejected call. Not a single emotion word. This is the camera test in practice.

B — Subtext (nobody says what they mean)

INT. CAR - MOVING - NIGHT

SARA drives. Passenger seat: empty bags, a laptop. LEO
(her brother, 30s, hasn't shaved in a week) stares out
the window.

                    SARA
          You eat today?

                    LEO
          There's a rest stop at exit 14.

                    SARA
          I wasn't asking about the rest stop.

A long beat. Highway lights sweep the glass.

                    LEO
          I had a granola bar.

Sara nods. Keeps driving.

Surface conversation: dinner. Real conversation: love and fear. Neither says the real thing. That gap is subtext.

C — Action Economy (one beat, one paragraph)

EXT. PARKING GARAGE - NIGHT

KENJI (20s, bike messenger gear, a bike that costs
more than his apartment) wheels to a stop.

Checks the address on his phone. Checks the building.

Takes out an envelope. Looks at it one beat too long.

Locks the bike. Goes in.

Each paragraph = one beat, none over two lines. The pause before "Goes in." lives in the white space above it.

D — The Dual Conversation (every good scene runs two at once)

INT. HOSPITAL CAFETERIA - DAY

CLAIRE (50s, still in scrubs) sits across from her
daughter NORA (20s, a duffel bag at her feet).

A tray of untouched food between them.

                    CLAIRE
          How was the drive?

                    NORA
          Fine. Roads were clear.

                    CLAIRE
          You didn't have to come all this way.

                    NORA
          I know I didn't.

Claire straightens a fork. Nora watches her do it.

                    CLAIRE
          The doctor says another week.

                    NORA
          I can stay a week.

A long silence. Claire looks at the fork.

                    CLAIRE
          You didn't bring much.

                    NORA
          I brought enough.

Surface: logistics. Real conversation: are you here because you want to be, or because you had to? Neither asks it. Both know the answer. Study question for every scene you ever read: find both conversations.

Open when you want the rules behind the four scenes above — ten lines, and they govern every scene you will ever writefold

The ten commandments

  1. Every scene is a negotiation: someone wants, someone resists, something changes. No change → cut.
  2. Enter late, exit early — every opening: "can I start three lines later?"; every ending: "can I end three lines earlier?" If yes, do it.
  3. Conflict = incompatible goals under pressure, not arguing.
  4. Dialogue is action in disguise; subtext = the gap between want and words.
  5. Show the flaw costing the protagonist something early (in a feature: by page 10).
  6. Cause-and-effect ("therefore / but"), never "and then."
  7. White space is pace: 3–4-line action blocks, verbs over adjectives.
  8. The reader's only question is "what happens next?" — end scenes mid-lean.
  9. Theme is the argument the plot conducts — give the counter-argument to a character you respect.
  10. Draft 1 is you telling yourself the story. Draft 3 is the first one that counts.

Keeping your work

5 min, once

A page that can be lost is not written yet.

Losing the file ends more first scripts than any craft problem. Five minutes now, against the only failure that cannot be rewritten.

How this file keeps things

Everything you type — scripts, ideas, cards, drill scores — is saved in this browser, on this computer, as you type. Nothing is ever sent anywhere: there is no account, no server and no network call in this file. That also means the file is only as safe as the browser it sits in. Clearing site data, a reinstall, or opening it in a different browser gives you an empty file.

So the rule is: download a backup, and once — only once — prove it restores. The backup is a single small .json file holding everything. Keep it in a cloud-synced folder (OneDrive, Drive, Dropbox) and a dead laptop costs you nothing.

The restore drill

Do this now, while there is nothing to lose:

  1. ⤓ Download a backup. It lands in your downloads folder.
  2. ✓ Test a backup file and pick it. This reads the file and reports what is in it, and changes nothing — it proves the file is real.
  3. Move it to your cloud folder. Do it again whenever you have written something you would be unhappy to lose. The file tells you how long it has been.

A backup is also how you move your work to another computer or another browser: download here, restore there.

And the script itself

Your pages are not trapped in here. Script ▾ exports:

  • .fdx — opens in Final Draft with the formatting intact.
  • .fountain — plain text that every screenwriting program reads, and that will still open in fifty years.
  • .txt and print → PDF — what you send to a reader.

Export the one you are working on at the end of any session that went well. Two copies in two places is the whole of file safety.

If you also write in Final Draft, do the same there: Preferences → General → Auto-Save on, backups pointed at a cloud-synced folder that is not your working folder, and one real restore before you trust it.

Catch an idea

The logline

Once the test passes · 5–10 min

The whole thing in one sentence, said aloud in twenty seconds.

Do: fill the formula. (A logline is your whole story in one sentence — the version you could say out loud to a stranger.)

When [disruption], a [flawed protagonist] must [concrete filmable goal] or [what's lost] — but [ironic obstacle].

"Backup": When the servers die the night before launch, a grieving engineer must restore from her dead mentor's laptop by morning or the company folds — but the restore erases the last work he ever wrote. "The Survey": When a burned-out survey agent accidentally reaches the most honest person in the city, he must finish his hundred calls by end of shift or lose his job — but she keeps exposing that every question on his script is a lie. "The Return": When a man returns to his hometown for the first time in twelve years, he must spend one hour in the childhood home before the auction — but his estranged sister is the listing agent holding the key.

Four failure modes:

  • Too long: more than two commas = two ideas — pick one
  • Too vague: "a story about grief" — a camera can't film "about"
  • No flaw: "a grieving person must…" beats "a person must…"
  • No irony: the best "but"s are where goal and obstacle are siblings

Logline and treatment, at feature scale

Formula: When [disruption], a [flawed protagonist] must [concrete goal] or [stakes] — but [obstacle/irony].

The people

Want, need, flaw — and the person who wants the same thing.

Do: three lines per character — Want (visible) · Need (hidden) · Flaw (the habit that costs them) — plus the opposition. (Full 7-element architecture for features → Character architecture — both sides, before outlining.)

Characterisation and true character — the distinction everything rests on

Use when: a character is vivid and the story still feels weightless, or a reader says "I don't know who this person is."

What it isWhere it lives
CharacterisationThe observable sum: age, job, dress, speech, style, IQ, the car they drive, how they take their coffeeEverything a camera can photograph in the first thirty seconds
True characterWhat they will actually do when it costs them somethingRevealed only by choice under pressure — and nowhere else

The rule. True character can only be expressed through choice in dilemma. Not choice — dilemma. A choice between good and evil is not a dilemma, it is a formality; nobody learns anything about a person who declines to commit a murder. Real dilemma is between two things they want, or two things they dread, and the deeper the pressure the deeper the revelation.

Two shapes of dilemma, both useful:

  1. Irreconcilable goods. Both options are genuinely desirable and they cannot both be had. Save the launch, or keep the last thing he ever made.
  2. The lesser of two evils. Both options are genuinely bad and one must be chosen. Tell her the truth and lose her, or keep her and become the thing you hate.

The diagnostic. Go through your outline and mark every point where your protagonist makes a decision. For each, ask: what did this cost them? If the honest answer is "nothing" more than once or twice, you have characterisation and no character — which is precisely the note that comes back as "I never really felt anything."

The corollary that saves rewrites. The best characterisation is a contradiction of the true character underneath it: the gentle man who is ruthless when it matters, the flippant woman who is the only one who stays. Design the surface to argue with the depth and the reader leans in to find out which one wins.

The vocabulary of change — the four units, smallest first

Use when: a scene "works" and you can't say why, or fails and you can't say why. These are Robert McKee's terms (Story, 1997) — worth learning because they are the words other writers and every development executive will use at you.

UnitWhat it isThe test you can actually run
BeatOne exchange of behaviour — an action and the reaction to itName each one as a verb pair: teasing / teasing back → insulting / insulting → threatening / daring → pleading / ridiculing → striking / leaving. Two consecutive beats with the same pair means one of them is doing nothing
SceneBeats in one place at one time that turn a value in a character's lifeWrite the value and its sign at the start and at the end — trust + → trust −. Same sign at both ends and it is not a scene yet, however good the dialogue is
SequenceTwo to five scenes culminating in a change bigger than any one of them deliversCould you cut any single scene and still get the sequence's change? Then cut it
ActSequences peaking in a change bigger than any sequence deliversAn act climax should be irreversible — a door that cannot be reopened

The value charge — the one diagnostic that never fails. Every scene runs on a value that can be positive or negative: trust, freedom, hope, life, love, truth, control. Write the value beside every scene in your outline with a + or − at the top and at the bottom. A script whose charges read + + + + + down the page is flat no matter how much happens in it; the whole art of pacing is the alternation. This is a ten-minute pass over a finished outline and it finds problems that a full read misses.

The gap. A character acts expecting the world to respond one way; the world responds another way. That mismatch — between expectation and result — is where story energy comes from, and it is why a scene in which everything goes as planned is dead on the page even when the plan is interesting. When a scene sags, the usual cause is that nobody's expectation has been violated.

The law of conflict. Nothing moves forward except through conflict. Not argument — conflict: two forces that cannot both get what they want. It has three possible addresses, and a strong scene usually works at least two of them at once: inner (a character against themselves), personal (against another person), extra-personal (against institutions, environment, time). If you cannot name which one a scene is running on, that is the note.

Exposition is not information; it is ammunition. The rule that follows from the law of conflict: never let a character say a fact simply because the audience needs it. Give the fact to someone who is using it to win something. A backstory delivered as a weapon in an argument is invisible; the same backstory delivered as explanation is the moment a reader starts skimming.

The fractal principle — why the same shape keeps appearing

John Yorke's argument in Into the Woods (2013) is the most useful single idea in structural theory, and it costs nothing to adopt: the shape of a whole film, an act, a scene, and even a good exchange of dialogue is the same shape. Someone wants something, meets resistance, and comes out of it knowing something they did not know going in. Stories are about a character acquiring knowledge — and every unit, at every scale, is a smaller copy of that acquisition.

What that buys you, practically:

  1. A structural problem at one scale is diagnosable at another. If your second act sags, look at whether your scenes have midpoints — a moment where the character glimpses the truth. They usually don't.
  2. You can practise the whole craft on one page. A single well-turned scene contains every structural principle you will ever need at feature scale. This is why the short is the right first project and not a consolation prize.
  3. The midpoint is not a plot device. At every scale it is the moment the character stops being able to un-know something. If your midpoint is merely an event, it is in the wrong place.

Companion lenses, worth being conversant with and not native in: Field's paradigm and plot points · the sequence method · Harmon's story circle · Truby's twenty-two steps · Vogler's twelve stages. Learn one model deeply, then read the others for the corrections they offer.

Scene headings — the three rules and the five parts

Use when: you are unsure whether this needs a new heading, or how much to put in one. Riley devotes his longest chapter to this because it is where beginners are most visible.

A new scene heading is required exactly three times. There is no fourth.

  1. The location changes. Farmhouse interior to farmhouse porch counts. Bedroom to kitchen counts.
  2. The time changes. Even in the same room: INT. LIBRARY - DEAD OF NIGHT and later INT. LIBRARY - MORNING.
  3. Visual logic demands it. Once you have narrowed to a shot the rest of the scene cannot be seen from — a close shot, a POV, an insert — you need a heading to get back out. BACK TO SCENE is legitimate and unglamorous.

Both failure modes are visible at a glance. Too few headings and the pages read like a short story — Riley's deadly mistake #6. Too many and the pages read like a writer auditioning to direct — deadly mistake #5. When in doubt, simpler is almost always better.

The economy rule, worth learning from the worked example. Riley's case: EXT. CEMETERY - CLOSE SHOT - RUSTED HANDGUN - DAY buries the one thing that matters inside repetition of a location and a time already established. The fix is to let the action deliver it —
EXT. CEMETERY - DAY → Grantham reaches between the broken bricks and pulls out: → RUSTED HANDGUN → The barrel is bent almost in half.

Capitals and parentheticals — the two rules writers break first

Capitalise inside action for three reasons and no others:

  1. A speaking character's first appearance — once, and only the first time.
  2. Sounds — capitalise the thing making the sound and the sound it makes. Sparingly, or the page starts shouting.
  3. Camera movement — which you should not be writing on a spec, so in practice there are two.

Some writers add a critical prop on its first significant appearance. Fine, very sparingly. Everything else stays in normal case, and a page peppered with capitals reads as anxious rather than emphatic.

Parenthetical character direction — the five rules:

  1. Keep it to a minimum. Most speeches should carry none at all.
  2. Never state the obvious. If context already shows she is furious, (furiously) insults the reader and the actor in the same breath.
  3. Lower case, no closing period, no "he" or "she". It goes on its own line — never inline with the dialogue.
  4. Never end a speech with one. If the beat belongs after the last word, it belongs in an action line.
  5. Keep it short. Riley's ceiling is four lines and he means it as an outer bound, not a target; a few words is the size of the thing.

The deletion test, which settles every case: delete it. If the line's meaning is genuinely unchanged, it was never earned. Legitimate survivors: the delivery contradicts the words · the speaker is addressing one person in a group · a tiny action beat · (beat).

Dialogue, subtext, compression

When the pages exist but feel flat · Read once, return every rewrite

Nobody says what they mean. Cut until it hurts, then cut the adverb.

Format makes a page professional. This step is what makes it good — and it is deliberately placed after you have written pages, because none of it means anything until you have flat dialogue of your own to hold it against.

Ten principles of dialogue

  1. Every line does double duty — character AND plot; one job = cut candidate.
  2. People never say exactly what they mean — subtext is the gap between want and words.
  3. Learn the five subtext moves (the five subtext moves).
  4. Cut the first line of every speech on suspicion (throat-clearing).
  5. Speeches rarely over 4 lines.
  6. No on-the-nose emotion statements — show behavior.
  7. Silence and behavior register as dialogue.
  8. Differentiate voices by rhythm and vocabulary (Character voice — four differentiators, and the test) — the cover-the-names test.
  9. Parentheticals are budgeted (deletion test → Format — the rulebook).
  10. Dialogue is scored — line lengths, interruptions, silences are tempo.

Dialogue is not conversation

Use when: the lines are "realistic" and the scene is dead.

Recorded real speech is repetitive, digressive, full of filler and almost entirely without direction. Screen dialogue only has to sound like speech while doing four jobs no real conversation does: it goes somewhere, it compresses, it withholds, and it is scored for tempo. Six mechanics that convert one into the other:

MechanicWhat to do
Put the key word lastScreen dialogue is periodic where it can be: the meaning lands on the final word, so the cut, the reaction and the next line all have somewhere to sit. "It wasn't the money I minded" beats "I didn't mind about the money."
Cut the greeting, cut the goodbyeNobody says hello on screen unless the hello is the scene. Same at the end: leave on the change.
Give each character a different tempoNot different vocabulary — different rhythm. One answers before you finish; one takes a beat every time; one never uses two clauses. Voice is heard before it is understood.
Let them fail to answerThe most useful line in any exchange is a reply to a question nobody asked. Real people dodge constantly and audiences read the dodge instantly → the five subtext moves.
Use interruption as punctuationA dash mid-line does more for pace than any adverb, and it tells you who has the power in the room.
Trust silenceA held look, an object picked up and put down, a beat of nothing — these register as dialogue and they cannot be misdelivered by an actor.

The test, and it takes four minutes. Read one page of your dialogue aloud at performance speed, then read a page of a produced script you admire the same way. You will hear the difference before you can name it — and what you are hearing is almost always compression, not talent. Then run Compression — the single most transferable skill on the page.

The five subtext moves

Move What it is Example
Deflection Answer a different question "Did you take the money?" / "I made dinner."
Attack Counter with an accusation "How could you?" / "You should ask yourself that."

The test: cover all dialogue — is the conflict visible in behavior? Uncover — is the dialogue saying something different from what the behavior implies? That gap = subtext working.

Character voice — four differentiators, and the test

  1. Vocabulary range — how many different words vs. the same ones recycled?
  2. Sentence length — long flowing clauses or short stops?
  3. What they never say — the absence of certain words is as distinctive as their presence.
  4. How they handle silence — fill it, fear it, create it?

Cover-the-names test: physically cover every character cue while reading. Can't tell who's speaking by the line alone? The voices aren't differentiated yet.

What was actually removed: the restatement of the slug ("This BMW showroom") · the editorialising ("a rare event indeed") · the staged reveal of a name the sentence could simply carry · four separate sentences of appearance collapsed into one list. Nothing that a camera could photograph was lost.

Four cuts that work on any page, in this order:

  1. The restatement. Anything the scene heading already said.
  2. The adverb. "Walks quickly" is "hurries". Stephen King's line — the road to hell is paved with adverbs — is a joke about a real reflex: the adverb is usually there because the verb is weak.
  3. The throat-clear. The first line of a speech, and the first sentence of an action paragraph, are both suspects by default. Cut them and see what breaks.
  4. The instruction. "begins to", "starts to", "proceeds to", "we see", "we watch". She does not begin to open the door. She opens the door.

The arithmetic that makes rewriting finite. Stephen King's formula in On Writing — second draft = first draft minus ten per cent — is arbitrary and astonishingly reliable. Set the target in advance, then find the words. If your rewrite came out longer than your draft, you did a different job than the one you meant to do.

Exposition — the hardest invisible skill

Use when: a scene has gone flat and you can feel yourself explaining, or a reader says the opening is "a bit slow."

Exposition is every fact the audience must have in order to follow the story — history, relationships, rules of the world. It is unavoidable, and handling it badly is the commonest reason a competent script dies on page eight. Six rules, in the order they matter:

  1. Convert it into ammunition. Never let a character state a fact because the audience needs it. Give the fact to someone who is using it to win something. Exposition delivered as a weapon in an argument is invisible; the same words delivered as explanation are the moment a reader starts skimming.
  2. Never let two people discuss what they both already know. If they both know it, they would not say it. Find the person in the room who doesn't know, or find a reason someone would say it anyway — cruelty, pleading, boasting, testing.
  3. Pace it across the whole film. The instinct is to front-load so the audience is comfortable. Resist it: an audience will happily be slightly confused if they are curious, and the confusion is doing the work. Hold your most important facts back as long as the story allows.
  4. Create the question before you give the answer. Every fact should arrive as the answer to a question the audience is already asking. A flashback nobody wanted is a stall; a flashback the audience has been begging for is a climax.
  5. Dramatise the dull part. Snyder's Pope in the Pool: if a scene must carry unavoidable plumbing, stage it inside something so interesting that nobody notices they are being informed.
  6. Cut the first line of every explanatory speech. It is nearly always a preamble telling the audience that information is coming. They can tell.

The search-and-destroy pass. Search your draft for "As you know", "Remember when", "You have to understand", "The thing is", "Ever since", "Let me explain" and "I've never told anyone this." Each hit is either the best line in the scene or a confession that you did not know how else to get the fact in. Usually the second.

Visual motifs — methodology

A motif = a concrete image that recurs with shifted meaning, always planted before it pays off.

  1. Pick a concrete object, action, or location with symbolic range.
  2. Plant it early, neutrally — so it registers without signaling.
  3. Return mid-story — same object, higher stakes.
  4. Return at climax or resolution — the meaning inverted or deepened.

Example: a dryer accepting quarters, one at a time. Scene 1: habit. Scene 3: she can no longer make change. Scene 5: someone else feeds the quarters for her. Three images, one idea, no dialogue required.

Finish, rest, rewrite

The day the last beat is drafted · Two weeks of calendar, a few hours of work

Finish it, leave it alone, then one pass per problem — never all at once.

The sequence: rest (≥24 h for a short; 10–14 days for a feature) → passes in order, one job per pass, ≥10 days cooling between major passes on long work → reader notes → pre-flight. (A pass = one full trip through the script fixing only one kind of thing — that focus is the entire trick of rewriting.) Draft and rewrite in different sessions, ideally different days.

Set the number before you start. Stephen King's formula — second draft = first draft minus ten per cent — is arbitrary, and it works. It converts an open-ended activity into a finite one, which is the actual problem with rewriting. A pass that comes out longer than what it started with was a different job than the one you meant to do. (The compression moves that get you there are in Dialogue, subtext, compression.) His other rule belongs here too: write with the door closed, rewrite with the door open — nobody sees a draft until it is finished, and then the right people see it early.

Getting notes that are usable — never send pages with "thoughts?" Send the seven questions:

  2. Where were you confused?
  3. What is the story about — one sentence?
  4. Which character would you cut?
  5. Which character do you want more of?

ACCEPT The note is right Execute it exactly

How to read a script

The other half of learning this. Read like a writer, not an audience.

The parallel-read drill, weekly

Pick one scene from a produced script. Read it → watch the filmed version → note three things:

  1. What changed between page and screen?
  2. What did the page give the director you can see on screen?
  3. What did the director add that wasn't on the page?

This trains you to see the page as a production document — exactly how a professional reader evaluates yours.
