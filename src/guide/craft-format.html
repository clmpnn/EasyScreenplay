Format — the rulebook

While typing pages, and before anyone sees them · Read once, consult forever

Every rule that decides whether a page looks professional.

Format is not taste, and it is not negotiable. It is the one part of this craft with a right answer, which makes it the cheapest professional signal you will ever buy — and the fastest way to be dismissed on page one if you get it wrong.

Authority: Christopher Riley, The Hollywood Standard, 3rd edition (2021) — the current edition; there is no 4th. Cross-checked against Final Draft's own guidance and the published rules of the Nicholl, Austin Film Festival and CineStory.

Scene headings — the three rules and the five parts

Use when: you are unsure whether this needs a new heading, or how much to put in one. Riley devotes his longest chapter to this because it is where beginners are most visible.

A new scene heading is required exactly three times. There is no fourth.

  1. The location changes. Farmhouse interior to farmhouse porch counts. Bedroom to kitchen counts.
  2. The time changes. Even in the same room: INT. LIBRARY - DEAD OF NIGHT and later INT. LIBRARY - MORNING.
  3. Visual logic demands it. Once you have narrowed to a shot the rest of the scene cannot be seen from — a close shot, a POV, an insert — you need a heading to get back out. BACK TO SCENE is legitimate and unglamorous.

Both failure modes are visible at a glance. Too few headings and the pages read like a short story — Riley's deadly mistake #6. Too many and the pages read like a writer auditioning to direct — deadly mistake #5. When in doubt, simpler is almost always better.

Mechanics: all capitals · no ending punctuation · INT. and EXT. keep their periods · every other element separated by space hyphen space · a heading should rarely wrap to a second line and effectively never to a third. Never number your scenes — the production office does that when a script is locked.

The economy rule, worth learning from the worked example. Riley's case: EXT. CEMETERY - CLOSE SHOT - RUSTED HANDGUN - DAY buries the one thing that matters inside repetition of a location and a time already established. The fix is to let the action deliver it —
EXT. CEMETERY - DAY → Grantham reaches between the broken bricks and pulls out: → RUSTED HANDGUN → The barrel is bent almost in half.

Capitals and parentheticals — the two rules writers break first

Capitalise inside action for three reasons and no others:

  1. A speaking character's first appearance — once, and only the first time.
  2. Sounds — capitalise the thing making the sound and the sound it makes. Sparingly, or the page starts shouting.
  3. Camera movement — which you should not be writing on a spec, so in practice there are two.

Some writers add a critical prop on its first significant appearance. Fine, very sparingly. Everything else stays in normal case, and a page peppered with capitals reads as anxious rather than emphatic.

Parenthetical character direction — the five rules:

  1. Keep it to a minimum. Most speeches should carry none at all.
  2. Never state the obvious. If context already shows she is furious, (furiously) insults the reader and the actor in the same breath.
  3. Lower case, no closing period, no "he" or "she". It goes on its own line — never inline with the dialogue.
  4. Never end a speech with one. If the beat belongs after the last word, it belongs in an action line.
  5. Keep it short. Riley's ceiling is four lines and he means it as an outer bound, not a target; a few words is the size of the thing.

The deletion test, which settles every case: delete it. If the line's meaning is genuinely unchanged, it was never earned. Legitimate survivors: the delivery contradicts the words · the speaker is addressing one person in a group · a tiny action beat · (beat).

Page specs

The measured standard. Riley publishes the figures the production world actually uses; Final Draft's factory defaults sit about 0.2″ to the left of them. Both circulate, both are fine, and nobody will ever put a ruler on your left margin. What people do notice is line length — because that is what makes a page equal a minute.

Do not cheat the margins. It is Riley's third deadly mistake and he is unusually blunt about it: people who look at script pages for a living can see it, pages turn more slowly, reading becomes a chore, and readers pass. The solution to too many words is never a wider measure. It is fewer words.

Breaking pages

Use when: exporting, or wondering why a page looks subtly wrong. the page does most of this — but the writer owns the result.

  • Break between paragraphs and between speeches whenever you can.
  • If you must break inside one, break at the end of a sentence, never mid-sentence.
  • Breaking a speech: (MORE) at the foot, (CONT'D) beside the name at the top of the next page. Break before a parenthetical, never after. You never type either of these.
  • Never break immediately after a scene heading — carry the heading to the next page.
  • Never break immediately before a transition — keep it with the scene it closes.

These are the rules that make a page look professionally set even when the writing underneath is a first attempt — and they are the rules a beginner is least likely to know are being judged.
