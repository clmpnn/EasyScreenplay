10–30 min

Five beats for a short. The order things happen in.

Do: five beats for a short — a beat is one moment where something changes; five of them, in order, are your whole story's spine. (Feature 15-beat sheet → the feature map.)

"Backup": 1. Alarms at 2 a.m., red dashboards. 2. Nadia tries every other backup — all corrupt. 3. The only clean copy is on HIS laptop. 4. Screen: RESTORE? ALL LOCAL FILES WILL BE ERASED. Y/N. 5. She presses Y. "The Survey": 1. Marcus on call 67 of 100. 2. He runs the script; she answers with unexpected honesty. 3. She asks him a question back — he has no script for it. 4. Supervisor walks by; log the call or get fired. 5. He answers her question. Then logs it. "The Return": 1. Rental car, staring at a For Sale sign he recognizes. 2. He knocks; Grace answers, goes rigid. 3. She gives him the key — sets a timer. One hour. 4. He finds something that tells him she kept waiting. 5. He leaves before the hour is up. Or doesn't. The timer goes off either way.

Can't find the shape? Write the ending first, then walk backward: "what forced this?"

Open when you need to name what's wrong — beat, scene, sequence, act, smallest firstfold

The vocabulary of change — the four units, smallest first

Use when: a scene "works" and you can't say why, or fails and you can't say why. These are Robert McKee's terms (Story, 1997) — worth learning because they are the words other writers and every development executive will use at you.

UnitWhat it isThe test you can actually run
BeatOne exchange of behaviour — an action and the reaction to itName each one as a verb pair: teasing / teasing back → insulting / insulting → threatening / daring → pleading / ridiculing → striking / leaving. Two consecutive beats with the same pair means one of them is doing nothing
SceneBeats in one place at one time that turn a value in a character's lifeWrite the value and its sign at the start and at the end — trust + → trust −. Same sign at both ends and it is not a scene yet, however good the dialogue is
SequenceTwo to five scenes culminating in a change bigger than any one of them deliversCould you cut any single scene and still get the sequence's change? Then cut it
ActSequences peaking in a change bigger than any sequence deliversAn act climax should be irreversible — a door that cannot be reopened

The value charge — the one diagnostic that never fails. Every scene runs on a value that can be positive or negative: trust, freedom, hope, life, love, truth, control. Write the value beside every scene in your outline with a + or − at the top and at the bottom. A script whose charges read + + + + + down the page is flat no matter how much happens in it; the whole art of pacing is the alternation. This is a ten-minute pass over a finished outline and it finds problems that a full read misses.

The gap. A character acts expecting the world to respond one way; the world responds another way. That mismatch — between expectation and result — is where story energy comes from, and it is why a scene in which everything goes as planned is dead on the page even when the plan is interesting. When a scene sags, the usual cause is that nobody's expectation has been violated.

The law of conflict. Nothing moves forward except through conflict. Not argument — conflict: two forces that cannot both get what they want. It has three possible addresses, and a strong scene usually works at least two of them at once: inner (a character against themselves), personal (against another person), extra-personal (against institutions, environment, time). If you cannot name which one a scene is running on, that is the note.

Exposition is not information; it is ammunition. The rule that follows from the law of conflict: never let a character say a fact simply because the audience needs it. Give the fact to someone who is using it to win something. A backstory delivered as a weapon in an argument is invisible; the same backstory delivered as explanation is the moment a reader starts skimming.

Open when you wonder why the same shape keeps appearing at every scalefold

The fractal principle — why the same shape keeps appearing

John Yorke's argument in Into the Woods (2013) is the most useful single idea in structural theory, and it costs nothing to adopt: the shape of a whole film, an act, a scene, and even a good exchange of dialogue is the same shape. Someone wants something, meets resistance, and comes out of it knowing something they did not know going in. Stories are about a character acquiring knowledge — and every unit, at every scale, is a smaller copy of that acquisition.

What that buys you, practically:

  1. A structural problem at one scale is diagnosable at another. If your second act sags, look at whether your scenes have midpoints — a moment where the character glimpses the truth. They usually don't.
  2. You can practise the whole craft on one page. A single well-turned scene contains every structural principle you will ever need at feature scale. This is why the short is the right first project and not a consolation prize.
  3. The midpoint is not a plot device. At every scale it is the moment the character stops being able to un-know something. If your midpoint is merely an event, it is in the wrong place.

Companion lenses, worth being conversant with and not native in: Field's paradigm and plot points · the sequence method · Harmon's story circle · Truby's twenty-two steps · Vogler's twelve stages. Learn one model deeply, then read the others for the corrections they offer.

The feature map

Three-act map (~110-pp feature):

Act Pages Function
1 · Setup 1–25 Protagonist, world, flaw · inciting incident ~pp. 10–12 · debate · commitment / Break Into Two ~p. 25
2A · Rising 26–55 New world · obstacles · fun & games · midpoint raises/doubles the stakes
2B · Escalation 56–85 Bad guys close in · internal doubts · all is lost ~p. 75
3 · Resolution 86–110 Climax won with what they learned · confrontation · denouement · final image answers the opening image

The 15-beat sheet (learn as your native model):

Beat ~Page Beat ~Page
Opening Image 1 Midpoint 55
Theme Stated 5 Bad Guys Close In 55–75
Set-Up 1–10 All Is Lost 75
Catalyst 12 Dark Night of the Soul 75–85
Debate 12–25 Break Into Three 85

A second model, learned as a corrective: Craig Mazin's Scriptnotes 403 argues structure is a symptom, not a template — that a screenplay is a character's relationship with a central dramatic argument: an arguable thesis; an opening "acceptable imperfection"; an inciting incident that is an ironic disruption aimed at that character's specific vulnerability; an Act 2 that reinforces the false belief while introducing its counter-position; a midpoint of genuine doubt; a low point where they lose both the old worldview and the road back; and an Act 3 that proves the theme through action, not acceptance. Learn the 15 beats for fluency and this for judgement.

Companion lenses (conversant, not native): Sequence method · Harmon's story circle · McKee's value model · Yorke's five acts.

Anti-formula warning: internalize the functions, hide the scaffolding. If the formula disagrees with your story, the story wins.

Open when the shape won't come — ten families, and yours is one of themfold

The ten story families

Use when: choosing what to write, or diagnosing why a draft feels like several films at once. Blake Snyder's genres are not marketing categories — they are structural families, and knowing which one you are in tells you what an audience already expects. His formulation of the job: give them the same thing, only different.

FamilyThe engineThe trap
Monster in the HouseA sin, a confined space, a monster. The rest is run and hideNo house — if they can walk away, there is no film
Golden FleeceA road, a team, a prize. The real prize turns out to be internal growthEpisodes that don't connect: the incidents must change the hero, or it's a travelogue
Out of the BottleA wish granted to someone under the thumb — then the wish turnsLetting the underdog enjoy it too long. The rule is that nobody gets to succeed indefinitely
Dude with a ProblemAn ordinary person, an extraordinary circumstance, no special skillsGiving them skills. The more average the person, the bigger the story
Rites of PassageA life change that hurts. The monster is the pain itselfSolving it externally. These end in acceptance, not victory
Buddy LoveTwo incomplete halves who hate each other, need each other, separate, reuniteLetting them like each other on page one
WhydunitNot who did it — what the answer reveals about human natureMaking it a puzzle. The detective must find something dark, not just a name
The Fool TriumphantAn underdog against an establishment that deserves puncturingA fool who is actually competent
InstitutionalizedA group, its rules, and the cost of belongingForgetting that the group is the antagonist
SuperheroAn extraordinary person in an ordinary world that cannot understand themMaking the world adequate to them — the opposition has to be worthy

Use this at Is it a story yet?, not at Writing the pages. Naming the family before you outline tells you which beats your audience is already waiting for — which is exactly the knowledge you need in order to withhold one of them deliberately. Naming it after you have drafted tells you only that you wrote two films.

Open when you want the laws behind the map, namedfold

Eight laws worth knowing by name

Use when: diagnosing a script that is structurally correct and still not working. Snyder's names are silly and that is why they stick; the underlying notes are real and are given by development executives every day of the week.

The nameThe note underneath it
Save the CatGet the audience in sync with the protagonist from the very start — usually by showing them do one thing that costs them something. Not likeability: investment
The Pope in the PoolBackstory the audience needs and does not enjoy should be delivered during something so interesting that nobody notices they are being informed
Double Mumbo JumboAn audience will accept one piece of magic. Two unrelated ones strain it past breaking
Laying PipeIf setup takes more than about 25 pages, you have a problem the audience will feel as impatience
The Covenant of the ArcEveryone in the film changes except the antagonist. Refusal to change is what makes them the antagonist
Watch Out for That GlacierA threat that approaches too slowly is not a threat. Give the danger a clock and a face
Black VetDo not stack two clever ideas into one premise. One twist, executed
Keep the Press OutCutting away to the wider world drains the pressure from the room your story is actually in
