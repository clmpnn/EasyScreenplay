Readers

When the draft is clean enough to survive being read

What to ask, who to ask, and what to do with what they say.

You need two or three people who read you regularly — not one person who reads you once. A standing swap beats a favour: it is even, it is repeatable, and neither of you has to feel awkward asking again.

  1. Swap partners are the backbone. Writers' forums and communities, classmates from any course you take, and script-hosting sites all work. What matters is that they write too, so the trade is fair. A message that gets answered: "I'm building a body of short scripts and I want to get better faster. Want a standing monthly swap — your pages for mine, notes within a week, written only?"

    Written only, on purpose: a spoken note becomes a conversation you will win, and winning it teaches you nothing.

  2. Etiquette — all three matter. Give first. Make notes specific and page-referenced. Never argue a note in the moment: say thank you, write it down, decide a week later. The note you argue with is usually the one that was right.
  3. Public threads for the small stuff. A logline, or the first ten pages, posted where strangers will be blunt. You are not looking for praise; you are looking for the place where three different people stopped.
  4. Give notes on two scripts a month, minimum. This is not charity — diagnosing someone else's sagging second act is the best craft class available, and it is free. You will see your own habits in their pages before you can see them in yours.

On paid coverage. Paid reads and contest entries exist, and some are worth it once a script is genuinely finished. Treat them as calibration, never as a substitute for readers — and check the current price and the current rules yourself before paying, because both change every year. Nothing in this file quotes a fee for that reason.

The table read

Worth doing twice on anything longer than a short: once when the draft is standing, once after the rewrite.

One 60–90-minute call · four to six people · parts assigned in advance · you read no part. Your job is to watch: where they lean in, where they check their phones, which line gets a laugh you did not write and which gets silence you did. Record it with everyone's consent, because you will not remember it accurately.

Schedule it about three weeks out and send the script five days before. Any closer and people arrive having skimmed it; any further and they forget.

Afterwards, give it a week before you change anything. What you feel the night of a table read is not what the script needs.
