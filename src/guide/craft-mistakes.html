The twelve mistakes

20 min · Score: 12 clean = ready to show · 10–11 = fix the two · under 10 = one more pass

What a reader sees before they read a word. The doctor finds nine of them.

Twelve mistakes account for nearly everything that gets a script dismissed on page one, and every one of them is mechanical — which means every one of them is fixable in an afternoon by someone who knows the list.

These twelve are craft. The twelve presentation mistakes — the ones that cost you before a reader reaches your writing — are the twelve deadly mistakes — Riley’s list, in full. Run both. The practice page's doctor runs the nine of these a machine can see — plus Riley's presentation rules and the Dialogue, subtext, compression search-and-destroy list — against your actual pages, and names the rule and the step behind every hit. Three (#5 empty scene, #8 passive protagonist, #9 invisible antagonist) stay yours: the card finds them.

# Mistake Looks like Fix
1 Wall of gray Action blocks 6–10 lines without a break One beat per paragraph, ≤4 lines — white space is pacing
2 Camera direction "CLOSE UP ON," "We see Marcus walk…" Delete. "Maya's hands — the wedding ring, still on." No instruction needed
3 Inner state "She is furious," "He realizes…" Behavior only (the camera test): "She closes the folder. Doesn't look at him."
4 On-the-nose speech Feelings explained in essay-like monologue Subtext swap — what would they say instead of what they mean?
5 Empty scene Talk, but nothing changes Back to the card. Can't fill "different at the end"? Cut or merge
6 Late-arriving scene Greetings, arrivals, establishing chitchat Cut to the first line of the real negotiation
7 Overstayed ending Goodbyes, summaries, neat wrap-up End on the last line where something changed
8 Passive protagonist Things happen to them; they react, wait, are informed Every scene: something they actively make happen or prevent
9 Invisible antagonist Conflict stays abstract (grief, society) Give opposition a face, a plan, and a reason
10 Functional names ANGRY WOMAN, DELIVERY GUY A character with real lines gets a name. (A one-line bit part — the laundromat's YOUNG MAN — can keep a function.)
11 Wrong extension V.O. / O.S. swapped In the scene's here-and-now? O.S. Not? V.O.
12 Format hand-me-downs Typed (MORE)/(CONT'D), manual blank lines, inconsistent name spelling Delete — the page handles all of it

The twelve deadly mistakes — Riley’s list, in full

Use when: auditing before a submission. The twelve mistakes's first twelve are craft mistakes; these twelve are presentation mistakes, culled by Riley from scripts on their way to the recycling bin. They overlap deliberately — the overlaps are the ones that cost you twice.

#The mistakeWhy it costs you
1Getting cute on the title pagePhotographs, display fonts, coloured ink. The title page cannot sell the script but it can make a disastrous first impression before a word is read. Title · "Written by" · name · contact. Courier 12, black on white
2Faded print, wrinkled pagesCompetition is intense and neatness is free. The PDF equivalent is a bad export — check yours opens clean on a phone
3Cheating the marginsVisible to anyone who reads scripts for a living. Cut words instead
4Neglecting to search and destroyTypos on page one say the writer has not read their own script. Why should anyone else?
5Overusing shot headingsPages peppered with medium shots and tracking shots break the illusion and suggest you do not know where the writer's job ends
6Underusing shot headingsThe opposite failure: pages that read like prose. Both are diagnosable in ten seconds
7Headings that are unnecessarily longThey bury the one piece of information that matters
8Mishandling parenthetical directionInline with the dialogue, or dangling off the end of a speech. Both are wrong — Format — the rulebook
9Using too many wordsEvery word must contribute to character, visuals, action or attitude. Everything else is cut
10Using up all the oxygenNo white space. A reader facing a dense page groans before reading a word of it
11Writing too many pagesRiley's numbers: 100–110 preferred; 120 was once acceptable and now reads as long; 135 usually is not read at all. Most competitions and producers that publish a range say 95–110, which agrees
12Slipping into the past tense"Will stepped into her path" instead of "Will steps into her path". The clearest possible tip-off that a writer is new — and the easiest of all to search out and destroy
